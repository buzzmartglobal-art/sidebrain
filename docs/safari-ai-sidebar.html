The best AI sidebar for Safari in 2026 (and why most of them bill you twice)

Safari is where AI sidebars go to die: the extension APIs don't cooperate, the market is smaller, and the ones that do show up mostly charge a monthly fee to reach a model you may already be paying for.

The short version: there is no best Safari AI sidebar, only a best fit. If you want zero setup, a hosted sidebar is the right answer and its subscription is the price of that. If you already pay for a model and want the panel to spend that quota, the options narrow to a handful — and the one we build, Sidebrain, is the least finished of them. It is free while we test it.

Why Safari has almost no AI sidebars

Every AI sidebar wants the same piece of screen: a narrow column pinned to the right, alive while you move between tabs. In Chrome that is a supported extension surface — a panel API, a stable slot, a documented lifecycle. Safari's extension APIs are built around a different set of primitives: a toolbar button, a popover that closes when you click away, and scripts injected into the page. There is no equivalent slot a third-party extension can own for the whole browsing session.

The market does the rest. Safari is a minority desktop browser, so shipping for it means writing a second client for a smaller audience, paying for a developer account, and signing and notarising the build. Most teams look at that and ship Chrome only, or ship a Safari build that is a stripped copy. The sidebars that do exist tend to be small-team products that charge yearly, because supporting a niche browser is expensive and a monthly subscription is the easiest way to fund it.

So we draw the panel inside the page instead

Sidebrain is a userscript. You install the free Userscripts extension, drop one file into its folder, and the script renders its own panel as a fixed element in the page you are reading. No Xcode, no signing, no review queue — and the panel genuinely stays put while you scroll, because it is part of the document.

That choice buys capability and costs reliability, and both are worth naming. On the plus side: it docks where a toolbar popover cannot, it works on any site you allow, and updating it means replacing a file. On the minus side: it cannot appear on PDFs, image-only pages or pages that block injected scripts; a site that rewrites its own layout can fight the panel; and the Pro features need a small local Node service running on your Mac, which is the sort of thing a hosted product hides from you on purpose.

The form factor, both ways

What a Safari sidebar can do that a web app can't

  • Read the page you are on without copy-paste
  • Stay docked while you scroll and switch tabs
  • Answer without a second browser window or tab juggling

What none of them can do well on macOS

  • Survive a Safari update that changes extension behaviour
  • Work on PDFs and image-only pages
  • Avoid a second process — hosting or local — somewhere

Two questions worth asking any Safari sidebar

First: where does the history go? Ask it before you build up six months of reading inside a tool. If the answer is "the cloud", you are renting your own archive — check whether export exists and what happens to it if you stop paying.

Second: whose tokens am I spending? A sidebar either holds your key, uses a quota you already own, or sells you its own credits. All three are legitimate; only the third gets more expensive the more you use the thing, and only the third turns your reading history into a cost centre for someone else. Knowing which one you are in explains most of the pricing differences between these products.
